## My notes

Three major frameworks anchor AI security and governance: the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025, NIST AI RMF 1.0, and Google’s Secure AI Framework (SAIF). Together, they map specific technical vulnerabilities, organizational trustworthiness goals, and operational defense strategies.

1. OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025)

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 identifies the most critical security vulnerabilities in generative AI applications.
• LLM01: Prompt Injection. Crafted user or external inputs manipulate the model into bypassing instructions or executing unintended actions.
• LLM02: Sensitive Information Disclosure. Models inadvertently leak personally identifiable information (PII), API keys, or proprietary data in their responses.
• LLM03: Supply Chain. Compromised third-party pre-trained models, datasets, or plugins introduce vulnerabilities into the application.
• LLM04: Data and Model Poisoning. Attackers tamper with training, fine-tuning, or embedding data to inject backdoors, biases, or false outputs.
• LLM05: Improper Output Handling. Downstream systems accept unvalidated LLM outputs, leading to traditional exploits like XSS, CSRF, or SQL injection.
• LLM06: Excessive Agency. Models are granted overly broad permissions, tools, or autonomy, allowing them to perform damaging actions without oversight.
• LLM07: System Prompt Leakage. Attackers extract hidden system instructions, exposing internal business logic, security guardrails, or embedded credentials.
• LLM08: Vector and Embedding Weaknesses. Flaws in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines or vector databases enable data leaks, unauthorized access, or context poisoning.
• LLM09: Misinformation. Models generate plausible-sounding hallucinations or false data, creating legal, financial, or reputational harm.
• LLM10: Unbounded Consumption. Uncontrolled resource usage enables Denial of Service (DoS) or "Denial of Wallet" attacks through expensive, high-volume queries.

2. NIST AI RMF 1.0: Trustworthiness Characteristics

In Part 1 (Section 3) of the NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), NIST defines seven socio-technical characteristics that organizations must balance based on an AI system's context of use:
• Valid and Reliable. The system performs accurately for its intended purpose (validity) and operates consistently over time under expected conditions (reliability).
• Safe. The system does not endanger human life, health, property, or the environment under defined operating conditions.
• Secure and Resilient. The system resists unauthorized access or malicious manipulation (security) and maintains or restores functionality after adverse events (resilience).
• Accountable and Transparent. Roles and responsibilities for system outcomes are clearly assigned, while stakeholders receive appropriate disclosures about how the AI operates.
• Explainable and Interpretable. The mechanisms behind an AI output are understandable (explainability) and meaningful to the intended audience within its context (interpretability).
• Privacy-Enhanced. The system safeguards human autonomy, identity, and sensitive personal data throughout the entire AI lifecycle.
• Fair with Harmful Bias Managed. The system minimizes systemic, computational, and human-cognitive biases to prevent unjustified discrimination.

3. Google Secure AI Framework (SAIF): Six Core Elements

Google’s Secure AI Framework (SAIF) provides a conceptual blueprint spanning six core operational elements:
• Security Foundations: Extend existing zero-trust infrastructure, identity management, and supply-chain controls directly to AI models and data.
• Detection & Response: Integrate AI inputs, outputs, and telemetry into standard Security Operations Center (SOC) and incident workflows.
• Automated Defenses: Leverage automation and AI-driven tooling to accelerate threat detection and remediation speeds.
• Platform Harmonization: Standardize security policies, model registries, and governance tooling across multi-cloud and development environments.
• Control Adaptation: Refine risk mitigations continuously using red-teaming insights, feedback loops, and evolving incident telemetry.
• Risk Contextualization: Evaluate model security within the context of end-to-end business processes and data lineage rather than in isolation
